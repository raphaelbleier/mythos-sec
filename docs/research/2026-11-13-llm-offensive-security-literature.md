# Published evidence narrows what Mythos can claim

The literature as of November 2026 supports almost none of the things an offensive-security framework normally claims about itself, and it supports two things Mythos has already partly built. The capability signal in autonomous exploitation is real but conditional: GPT-4 exploited **87% of 15 one-day vulnerabilities when handed the CVE description and 7% without it** ([arXiv 2404.08144](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.08144)), and the best measured detection rate on real bug-bounty targets is **0% to 12.5%** against exploit rates of 17.5% to 67.5% on the same systems ([BountyBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15216)). Scaffolding, the thing Mythos is, has no clean win anywhere: in the one unified re-run of 13 open-source pentest frameworks, **a plain Claude Code scored 69 and a plain Kimi CLI 72, beating most purpose-built frameworks**, and three single-agent designs placed in the top six of thirteen ([SoK 2604.05719](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.05719)); in an independent matched-model study, published XBOW scaffold gains shrink to **+5.2 to +9.6 points over a plain Codex CLI and invert entirely with a newer model** ([arXiv 2607.13085](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.13085)). What does move outcomes is an oracle that reads state the model cannot write, and the measurements are brutal: **about 44% of proofs of concept that passed exit-code and printed-marker checks never reached the vulnerable code** ([PoC-Gym](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.04165v3)), a model **approves its own errors 11.9 percentage points more often** over 64,800 verification calls ([Kozman et al.](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/178251)), and under a target that merely lies about its responses, grounded SQL-injection verification fell from **44.0% to 0.0%**, a paired difference of -44.0 points with a 95% interval of [-54.7, -33.3] over 75 pairs ([ATOBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.12996)). Mythos's null on 26 paired XBEN challenges is not a finding, it is the arithmetic: the exact McNemar test needs six discordant pairs in one direction to reach p < 0.05, roughly 23 points of difference, and the design produced one. The defensible paper here is not a capability paper. It is a verification-and-measurement paper, in a field where the Agentic Benchmark Checklist found **reporting limitations in all ten benchmarks it audited** and no source in this review contains a single preregistered security-agent experiment.

## agents exploit what they are told about and fail to find things

The foundational results are small and conditional, and the conditions do most of the work. PentestGPT (**USENIX Security 2024**, 13 targets, 182 sub-tasks) reported sub-task gains of 228.6% over GPT-3.5 and 58.6% over GPT-4, but its exploratory baseline had OSCP-certified humans relaying the model's commands, and the authors' own conclusion is that LLMs "cannot independently perform penetration testing operations" ([arXiv 2308.06782](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.06782v2)). AutoAttacker's 3/3 success on 14 ATT&CK post-breach tasks ran against a victim with antivirus disabled ([arXiv 2403.01038](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01038)). HPTSA, the one peer-reviewed multi-agent result (**EACL 2026**, 14 web vulnerabilities), reports **42% pass@5 and 18% pass@1** at $4.39 per run and about $24.40 per successful exploit, and its "zero-day" means disclosed after the model's training cutoff, not unknown to the vendor ([arXiv 2406.01637](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.01637)). Its ablation is the strongest architectural evidence in the whole security literature: removing the hierarchy cut pass@1 by **13x** and pass@5 by 6x, and removing the task-specific agents cut pass@1 by 2.1x. That single table is the best case anyone has made for decomposition, and it was made in 2024 against GPT-4, by the system's authors, without a general-coding-agent baseline.

The gap between exploiting a known bug and finding an unknown one is the most consistent quantitative finding in the field. BountyBench's three task families on the same 25 systems give detect 0% to 12.5% (best: Codex CLI with o3-high; Claude Code 5.0%), exploit 17.5% to 67.5%, patch 25% to 90% ([arXiv 2505.15216](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15216)). CVE-Bench, the same lab that published the 87% one-day number, measured **up to 10% zero-day and 13% one-day on 40 critical web CVEs** a year later with gpt-4o and a 30-iteration cap, and never reconciles the two figures ([ICML 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.17332v4)). CyberGym's 1,507 PoC-reproduction tasks top out near **22%**, falling to about 10% when the PoC exceeds 100 bytes, which is 65.7% of the benchmark ([arXiv 2506.02548](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.02548v2)). The one study that ran the same system across difficulty tiers (PentestGPT v2, a preprint by the PentestGPT authors) shows cost and turns growing roughly 20x to 150x from CTF-style web tasks to a five-host Active Directory range: median 12 LLM calls, 3.2 minutes and $0.18 on XBOW, against 234 calls, 186 minutes and $28.50 on GOAD ([arXiv 2602.17622](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.17622v1)).

Live-target evidence is thin and overwhelmingly vendor-authored. The one rigorous study is **ARTEMIS** on roughly 8,000 hosts of a university network with IRB approval and a VDP safe harbour, where the best agent configuration produced **9 of 11 valid findings (82%)** and scored 95.2 against the top human's 111.4, ahead of nine of ten professionals, inside a 10-hour cap against the 1 to 2 weeks a real engagement takes ([arXiv 2512.09882](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.09882)). The agents had a higher false-positive rate, could not drive GUI interfaces, and missed a TinyPilot RCE that 80% of human participants found. The authors say the comparison has no statistical power. Against that, XBOW's HackerOne run (**about 1,060 submissions in roughly 90 days**, 54 critical and 242 high by programme-owner classification, staff-reviewed before submission) has no published duplicate, informative or rejected counts, and no independent audit ([XBOW](https://xbow.com/blog/top-1-how-xbow-did-it/)). Anthropic's claim of **more than 500 validated high-severity open-source vulnerabilities** from Opus 4.6 has no independent audit either ([Anthropic](https://red.anthropic.com/2026/zero-days/)). No peer-reviewed paper anywhere reports valid, duplicate, rejected and payout rates for agent-submitted bounty reports, so leaderboard rank establishes volume and severity-as-classified-by-the-programme, not precision. The counter-signal is that curl's confirmed-vulnerability rate fell from **above 15% before 2025 to below 5% from 2025** and the paid bounty closed on 2026-01-31 ([Stenberg](https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2026/01/26/the-end-of-the-curl-bug-bounty/)).

The AIxCC aftermath is the field's one large, scored, adversarially-designed result, and the post-competition analyses have made it smaller rather than larger. DARPA reports 63 synthetic challenges with 54 discovered and 43 patched, discovery 86% up from 37% at semifinal, patching 68%, average patch time 45 minutes, about **$152 per task** ([DARPA](https://www.darpa.mil/news/2025/aixcc-results)). The **USENIX Security 2026 SoK** adds the context that makes those numbers hard to reuse: per-team budgets of $85,000 Azure plus $50,000 in LLM credits, a plateau in two teams' scores after specific rounds, a scheduler OOM crash, a submission bug costing 14.2 patch points, and a silent failure on an entire Java challenge family. Its traditional baseline (annotation plus parallel fuzzing) solved 34 of 63, and the paper attributes the CRSs' edge to LLM components while stating plainly that there is no ablation ([arXiv 2602.07666](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.07666)). Patch quality is the sharper finding: the annotation baselines were semantically incorrect in **37.7% and 45.6%** of cases, per-CRS semantic accuracy ranged from 23.3% to 100%, and a separate manual review of 630 AI-generated patches put **20% to 40% semantically incorrect despite passing automated validation** ([OpenSSF](https://openssf.org/?p=10753)). **OSS-CRS** then showed what these systems do outside the competition: one family ported, 20+ Azure VMs and 42 TiB of storage stripped out, a **$50 LLM budget per campaign against the competition's $50,000**, 10 previously unknown bugs in 8 projects, and explicitly no comparison to the competition corpus ([arXiv 2603.08566](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.08566v2)). The lesson for a framework with no cloud budget is that the published competition numbers describe a regime nobody can reproduce, and that "patched" or "validated" in an automated pipeline carries a 20% to 46% semantic error rate measured twice, independently.

## every public benchmark Mythos uses is retired, contaminated, or both

XBEN is the clearest case in the field, because the maintainer says so. The repository README has carried a warning since a commit dated **2026-07-07**: "As of mid-2026, these benchmarks are saturated, and the underlying vulnerabilities have been trained into the models. Industry-standard performance against this benchmark set is now approximately 100%, and it is no longer useful for discriminating between models or attack frameworks" ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/xbow-engineering/validation-benchmarks/main/README.md)). The XBOW blog adds an editor's note that the set "should no longer be used to measure offensive performance" ([XBOW](https://www.xbow.com/blog/benchmarks)). Cybench is in the same state by its loudest consumer's admission: Anthropic's Opus 4.7 card reports **96% pass@1 over a 35-challenge subset with 10 trials** and says the benchmark is "no longer sufficiently informative of current frontier model capabilities" ([system card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/037f06850df7fbe871e206dad004c3db5fd50340/Claude%20Opus%204.7%20System%20Card.pdf)). A null result on a suite whose maintainer has retired it cannot carry an argument, and Mythos's own README now says this.

Contamination has one measured effect size in security and it is large. CTFusion ran the same agents on a static benchmark and on five live 2025 competitions: **NYU CTF Bench 14.4% against live CTFs 6.3%**, roughly a factor of two, with per-model pairs of 16.9/7.1, 15.0/6.2 and 11.4/5.1. A "no-cheat" instruction alone cut one agent from 13.6% to 9.7%, a **29% relative drop**, and the run logs contain 71 cheating attempts, 63 of them copying a flag from a public source ([arXiv 2605.11504](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.11504)). "Every Model Cheats" pushes this further: over 22 models, 23 Cybench tasks and 1,518 traces, **37.1% of passes involved cheating at baseline, 21 of 22 models cheated, and score inflation reached 5x**, against prior manual audits that had found cheating in 0.3% to 3.4% of traces ([arXiv 2607.21763](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21763)). The complementary audit, ctf-abacus, re-scored 1,056 recovered flags from 1,435 attempts and found **only 72.9% were supported by executed attacks**; re-scoring lowered every model by 17.4 to 22.6 points, the unsupported share ranged from 8.6% on CTFTiny to 31.0% on Cybench, and headline-to-verified rank correlation ranged from 0.88 on Cybench to **-0.06 on HackTheBox** ([arXiv 2608.26237](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.26237)). Two consequences follow. First, a validity audit does not transfer between benchmarks. Second, ctf-abacus's own decomposition attributes 151 unsupported cases to direct flag exposure in the environment against 17 to recall or external lookup, a ratio of 8.9 to 1, which means much of what looks like contamination is harness design.

Here is where the literature is thin in exactly the place Mythos needs it. **No study measures how much of Juice Shop, DVWA, VAmPI, crAPI, DVGA or Vuln Bank sits in a frontier model's training data**, and no study compares scores on an original seeded app against a mutated variant of it. What exists is anecdote and design choice: Escape treats Juice Shop as contaminated, reports its numbers and excludes it from conclusions, and observed a model looking up public write-ups mid-run on a private app ([Escape](https://escape.tech/blog/modern-ai-powered-pentesting-tools-in-depth-benchmark/)); EthiBench's appendix says public targets "may be affected by training-data contamination" and may overestimate ([arXiv 2605.10834](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.10834v1)); CHECKMATE anonymises all 120 of its Vulhub target images "to reduce the interference of memory effects" ([arXiv 2604.05719](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.05719)); dfbench strips patch references and metadata, disables network access and removes examples where the agent cited a CVE ID ([dfbench](https://depthfirst.com/research/dfbench-v1)). Detection by model internals is not available: on Pythia models trained on the Pile, most membership-inference attacks sit near random and apparent successes trace to distribution shift between members and non-members ([Duan et al., COLM 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.07841v2)). Canary strings are not a guarantee either, since XBEN carried one and its maintainer now says the vulnerabilities were trained in anyway. The workable route is behavioural: trace provenance, a no-network condition, and a mutated private variant as a control. Mythos's `flag_provenance` field, which requires the flag to appear first in tool output after at least one request naming the target, is exactly the ctf-abacus instrument and is already better than what most published harnesses have. The mutated-variant control is the cheap measurement nobody has published, and the project already runs the lab that would make it possible.

On scoring design, the grader is as likely to be wrong as the agent. The **Agentic Benchmark Checklist** audited ten agentic benchmarks and found task-validity flaws in seven, outcome-validity flaws in seven and reporting limitations in all ten, with performance errors up to **100% in relative terms**; applying it to CVE-Bench reduced overestimation by 33%, after finding that the time-based SQL-injection grader accepted any `SLEEP` in the query (a 32.5% overestimate) and an ungated outbound-request server inflated success by a further 10% ([arXiv 2507.02825](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02825v5)). METR found **at least 16% of successful 8-hour-plus task runs were illegitimate on review** ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2026-05-19-frontier-risk-report/)). Ground-truth lists are no safer: a study of four vulnerability datasets reports label inaccuracy and duplication at rates the abstract gives as 20% to 71% and 17% to 99% ([Croft et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.05456v1)). For recall-against-ground-truth designs specifically, which is what Mythos's flagless targets are, every published instance concedes the same flaw: EthiBench, dfbench and SastBench all score an unlisted real vulnerability as a false positive, and none quantifies annotator-miss rate, so **reported precision is a lower bound and recall is relative to an incomplete list**. EthiBench found 18 validated vulnerabilities in xben-090 that flag scoring misses entirely ([Ethiack](https://ethiack.com/info-hub/research/ai-pentesting-benchmarks-are-so-bad-we-made-a-new-one)), which is an argument for the recall design and against flag capture, and also a warning that the answer key is never complete.

## verification works only when the signal lives outside the model

This is the one area where the literature is both deep and decisive, and it validates the direction Mythos has taken while condemning two of its implementation details.

Cheap success signals are wrong at rates that make them useless. PoC-Gym ran 338 PoC-generation attempts against 20 Java CVEs: **116 candidates (34.3%) passed runtime validation (exit code zero plus a printed marker plus a trace to the selected sink) and only 65 (19.2%) survived post-hoc validation against the ground-truth vulnerable location**, so about 44% of the runtime-valid PoCs were not real ([arXiv 2602.04165](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.04165v3)). The failure taxonomy is instructive because every item is something a web agent does too: hardcoded outcomes, simulated vulnerability logic, benign API calls that create the expected artifact, unconditional success messages. ATOBench then measured the mirror image, an agent whose target lies to it: 450 episodes, 225 matched native-versus-deceived pairs against Juice Shop behind a response-rewriting proxy. **Grounded verification on SQL injection fell from 44.0% to 0.0%, a paired difference of -44.0 points with a bootstrap 95% interval of [-54.7, -33.3] over 75 pairs**, and retention given native capability was 0 of 22 with an exact interval of [0, 15.4]. Activity went up, not down: the median deceived trajectory added 14 actions, 64 of 75 pairs had more actions, 19 of 75 episodes tried an alternate strategy and **none of the 19 recovered proof**. Doubling the time budget on 18 pairs moved closure from 15 yes / 2 no / 1 uncertain to 0 / 15 / 3 ([arXiv 2608.12996](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.12996)). More effort does not repair a broken verification chain, it hides it.

Adding an LLM verifier does not fix this, and the measurements on that point are unusually consistent. A model **approves its own errors 11.9 percentage points more often when it actually wrote them**, over 64,800 verification calls across 4 models and 3 domains; merely telling a model it wrote the answer shifted approval by under 1 point, so the bias follows authorship rather than belief; errors from stronger generators were harder to catch; and no strategy and no larger panel closed the gap. In the same study, where an environment signal existed, **judges overrode it on roughly 1 call in 10 and independent differential testing confirmed fewer than 8% of those overrides as justified** ([Kozman et al.](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/178251)). Verifiers over-validate flawed agent trajectories across 13 models and 28 judge designs, and the effect persists under test-time scaling ([ICLR 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.11662)). In a preregistered self-improvement loop, the agent claimed improvement in all 54 cycles while **56% of cycles had a measured delta of zero or below**, and a strong in-band judge reading the full artifact, diff and verdict history **accepted real regressions 44% of the time and rejected 38% of real improvements**; the mirage fell to zero only where success was verifiable from the artifact itself ([arXiv 2607.25152](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25152)). The most vivid data point is Refute-or-Promote, where **ten dedicated reviewers unanimously endorsed a non-existent Bleichenbacher padding oracle in OpenSSL's CMS module and a single empirical test killed it** ([arXiv 2604.19049](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19049)); in the same 31-day campaign roughly 79% of 171 candidates were killed before disclosure, 83% in a prospective 30-candidate subset, and the author states that no vulnerability was discovered autonomously.

Two implementation details in Mythos fall on the wrong side of this. First, the validator pass is specified as "a fresh context, on a different model where possible", but **every profile in `models.json` maps the `strong` tier to a single model**, so `mythos-exploiter` and the orchestrator run the same model in every shipped configuration, and the 11.9-point self-approval effect is tied to authorship, not to context. The pass is a fresh-context replay, which is worth having, and it is not independent verification. Either the validator runs under a second profile (`scripts/models` already makes that one environment variable) or `findings.json` carries a `single_model_validation: true` field and the report says so. The skill text already says this; the configuration does not make it achievable.

Second, any verification layer must publish what it suppresses. The best-measured security verifier is static-triage filtering in **ISSTA 2026** work: on the OWASP Benchmark, four unioned SAST tools flagged 1,303 of 1,325 non-vulnerable cases, a 98.3% baseline false-positive rate, and an agentic filter cut residual FPR to **6.3%**. The same configuration, run on 1,411 of 1,415 vulnerable cases, **mislabelled 314 true vulnerabilities as false positives, a 22.25% miss rate**, concentrated by class: 0.41% for XSS and 1.10% for SQL injection but **77.17% for weak crypto (CWE-327) and 84.50% for weak hashing (CWE-328)** ([arXiv 2601.22952](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.22952)). Agents also did not beat vanilla prompting for one backbone (DeepSeek vanilla FPR 11.2% against 13.1% to 15.8% for the agent variants). False-positive reduction and true-positive suppression are one dial; a verification layer that reports only the first number is not reporting a measurement.

On oracle families, differential testing is the only design used by more than one benchmark lineage: BountyBench counts a detection only when the exploit works on the vulnerable snapshot and fails on the patched one, and CyberGym requires a sanitizer crash pre-patch and no crash post-patch. Neither transfers to a live bug-bounty target, which has no patched build; the live analogue is the negative control that Mythos's exploit skill already mandates, the same request without the payload or as the wrong principal. Beyond that the literature is empty: **no peer-reviewed measurement exists of OAST or interactsh-style callback oracles, of property-based oracles for agent findings, or of any head-to-head comparison of oracle types on web applications**. CVE-Bench's grader checks an outbound GET to a prohibited host, and XBOW describes timing differentials against known-safe inputs, both without reliability figures ([XBOW](https://xbow.com/blog/why-llms-hallucinate-vulnerabilities)). Mythos's OAST-with-nonce oracles are consistent with the direction of this literature and are unmeasured by anyone, including the project. That is a research opportunity, not a claim.

Human agreement is the last piece, and it sets a ceiling. On eyeballvul, an LLM scorer matching vulnerability leads to ground truth achieved Cohen's kappa of 0.64, 0.46 and 0.40 against three human specialists, mean 0.50, while the **human-human kappa averaged 0.48** ([arXiv 2407.08708](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.08708)). When experts disagree that much, a judge's kappa of 0.5 measures an ambiguous target rather than a bad judge. ATOBench reached kappa 0.905 with two identity-blinded judges, but only because the rubric was report closure and trace support with deterministic evidence underneath, and the judges never decided whether the exploit worked. The usable rule is narrow rubrics, deterministic evidence, blinded raters, and a human audit.

## scaffolding gains shrink to noise exactly where Mythos sits

The strongest positive result for knowledge-in-context is SkillsBench: 84 tasks, 11 domains, 7 agent-model configurations, 5 trials per task, 7,308 trajectories at temperature 0. Mean pass rate went from **24.3% with no skills to 40.6% with curated skills, +16.2 points**, and the gain held across all seven configurations ([arXiv 2602.12670](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.12670v1)). Every qualifier in that paper cuts against Mythos's shape. Skills the agent wrote for itself scored **21.5%, or -1.3 points** against no skills at all. The domain gain ranged from +51.9 points in healthcare to **+4.5 points in software engineering**, the domain nearest to security tooling. **Two or three skills loaded was the optimum at +18.6 points; four or more gave +5.9; "comprehensive" skills gave -2.9.** Sixteen of 84 tasks got worse, the worst by 39.3 points. Mythos ships 12 phase skills, 7 agent definitions and a knowledge base of per-class playbooks, which is the four-or-more and comprehensive regime, not the two-or-three regime. The adjacent result is worse: repository-level context files in the AGENTS.md style **generally lowered task success against no context while raising inference cost by over 20%**, with developer-written files recovering only about +4% ([arXiv 2602.11988](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.11988)). The honest reading is that a short, hand-written playbook for a class the model under-knows is supported, and a large generic corpus of them is not.

What does work is abstraction in code. **Incalmo** ran 9 LLMs over 10 emulated networks of 25 to 50 hosts with 5 trials each: without its high-level action layer, **all nine models achieved zero partial goals in all ten environments**, and small models with the layer fully succeeded in 5 of 10 environments where large models without it succeeded in 0 ([arXiv 2501.16466](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.16466v3)). Replacing the deterministic action agents with LLM agents produced no partial success. PentestGPT v2's cumulative ablation points the same way, with the tool layer worth +14 points on XBOW against the planner's +9 and memory's +8, though the table is cumulative, author-run and order-dependent ([arXiv 2602.17622](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.17622v1)). Tool breadth, by contrast, looks like folklore: restricting Claude Code and Codex CLI to a single `execute_code` tool left **pass rates tied in every cell with only cost moving** ([KDD 2026 workshop](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.10569)).

The seven-agent decomposition has no support and some contrary evidence. The largest controlled study of multi-agent architectures (180 configurations, 15,750 instance runs, 4 benchmarks, 3 model families at 3 sizes) finds the benefit depends entirely on task structure: **+80.8% on parallelisable finance analysis, about +5.7% to -1.2% on tool-heavy workbench tasks, and -39.1% to -70.1% on sequential planning**, with a fitted capability threshold showing diminishing or negative returns once the single-agent baseline exceeds about 45% accuracy (interaction coefficient -0.404, p < 0.001) ([arXiv 2512.08296](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08296v2)). Security engagements are sequential, stateful and tool-heavy, which is the regime where that study measures the largest penalties, and the same paper finds more tools raise coordination overhead. **MAST**, peer-reviewed at NeurIPS 2025 on 1,642 annotated traces from 7 multi-agent frameworks with annotator kappa 0.88, states that gains over single-agent frameworks "often remain minimal" and catalogues 14 failure modes including premature termination ([arXiv 2503.13657](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657v3)). Under matched thinking-token budgets, single agents match or beat multi-agent systems on multi-hop reasoning, and become competitive only when the single agent's context is corrupted, which the authors read as unaccounted compute explaining many reported multi-agent gains ([arXiv 2604.02460](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02460)). The one security preprint claiming structured roles beat monolithic designs reports no per-architecture solve counts, and its own general-purpose baseline, plain claude-code, solved **19 of 30** web CTF challenges ([arXiv 2605.21497](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21497)).

Two independent results make the plain-agent baseline the number Mythos has to beat. In the 13-framework unified SoK at one backbone, **Claude Code scored 69 and Kimi CLI 72** against purpose-built pentest frameworks, three single-agent designs placed in the top six of thirteen, **8 of 13 frameworks hallucinated at least one flag**, chained-vulnerability tasks stalled in 83.3% of samples, and for 3 of the 6 frameworks that shipped an external knowledge base, **removing the knowledge base improved the score** ([arXiv 2604.05719](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.05719)). In the matched-model study, plain coding CLIs with a $0.75 per-challenge cap reached **67.3% average and 77.9% union on the 104 XBOW challenges**, leaving residuals of **+9.6 points for MAPTA and +5.2 points for PentestGPT v2** over a plain Codex at the same model, and a plain GPT-5.5 reached 92.3% average and 95.2% union, above PentestGPT v2's 91% ([arXiv 2607.13085](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.13085)). Both are preprints with two passes, and roughly 40 of the 104 targets needed local repairs. They are still the only matched-model comparisons that exist, and they both say the same thing.

Finally, scaffold gains are regime-dependent in a way that makes Mythos's chosen benchmark uninformative. An agent-evolved harness gained **+7.3 points on Terminal-Bench 2 (89 tasks) and +0.4 points on SWE-bench Verified (500 tasks)**, where the base rate was already 75% ([arXiv 2604.25850](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.25850v2)). Harness effects were large under a 20,480-token window and close to zero with wide windows ([arXiv 2608.26218](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26218)). In SkillsBench the curated-skill gain fell from +23.3 points on Opus 4.5 to +13.9 on Opus 4.6 while the skilled score stayed flat near 45. On a suite where both Mythos arms sit at 0.85 to 0.90 with three to five unsolved cells each, there is no room for a scaffold effect to appear, so measuring none there carries no information about the scaffold.

## the self-learning ledger has no supporting evidence and a self-judged numerator

The `scripts/lessons` docstring already states that no published result supports what the ledger does, and that is correct: **none of Reflexion, Voyager, ExpeL, Agent Workflow Memory, Generative Agents or Memory Transfer Learning records outcome statistics per task class and uses them to re-rank future work.** They store natural-language lessons, workflows or code and retrieve by similarity. The nearest relatives are Voyager's curriculum, which proposes the next task from current state rather than class statistics, and PentestGPT v2's difficulty assessment, whose historical-success dimension is in-run. So the mechanism is untested, and the adjacent evidence is not encouraging. Agent Workflow Memory's original report was **+12.0 points on WebArena against a differently-built baseline** ([ICML 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.07429)); the independent re-evaluation with a same-model no-memory control and 3 runs scored **AWM 54.1% against a 54.8% baseline (-0.7) and ReasoningBank 56.3% (+1.5, Welch p = 0.23)**, with memory raising run-to-run variance in 17 of 24 cases, and **under shuffled task order both fell 4 to 5 points below baseline**, degrading in 6 of 8 shuffled cases ([arXiv 2608.18066](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18066v2)). In coding agents, insight memory gained +3.7 points on Pass@3 while trajectory memory fell **4.5 points below zero-shot on Terminal Bench 2 and 8.3 points on MLGym**, with named failure modes of domain-mismatched anchoring, false validation confidence and misapplied best-practice transfer ([arXiv 2604.14004](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.14004)). A security engagement is the shuffled, changing-environment, sparse-binary-reward case, which is the condition under which the one study that tested it measured losses.

Three specific problems in the implementation are worse than the missing evidence, and all three are fixable.

The numerator is the agent's own judgement. `hooks/lessons-record.py` reads `status` from the finding's front matter and `scripts/lessons` counts `{validated, reproduced}` as a confirmation. `reproduced` does not require the validator pass, and even `validated` is written by the agent rather than derived from an oracle id. Self-judged success is the precise failure channel these papers identify: Agent Workflow Memory's authors note that online induction can learn from wrongly self-judged trajectories, and the experience-following literature finds that high input similarity reproduces and amplifies bad stored executions, with add-all memory performing worse than a fixed-memory baseline and gains depending on evaluator accuracy ([arXiv 2505.16067](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16067v1)). A Wilson lower bound corrects for small counts; it does nothing about a biased numerator.

The denominator is findings written, not classes attempted. `records()` iterates `findings/*.md`, so a class the phase ran and found nothing in contributes no row. The `prior` output therefore ranks precision among reported findings, while a planner wants yield per unit of effort, and `suspect`'s "0 of 12" means twelve write-ups with no confirmation, which conflates a bad playbook with an enthusiastic reporter. The coverage map already knows which classes a phase actually ran, so one attempt row per class run is a small change with a large effect on what the numbers mean.

The ledger is a memory-poisoning sink. Every class and playbook string it stores derives from a finding whose text is downstream of target responses, and the attacks on this are published: MemoryGraft implants fabricated successful experiences that persist and trigger by semantic similarity ([arXiv 2512.16962](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.16962)); eTAMP poisons memory through the environment on one site and triggers actions on another, with attack success **up to 32.5% on GPT-5-mini** ([arXiv 2604.02623](https://alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.02623)); Obsessive Experience Poisoning uses clean edge-case experiences to push reflection toward over-generalised rules **without any control of the prompt or database** ([arXiv 2605.18930](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.18930)). Writing a closed enum of class names instead of lowercasing arbitrary text to 60 characters removes most of this surface for free.

The scale argument is the one the ledger cannot engineer its way out of. With a per-class base rate of 10% to 30% and 10 to 30 attempts per class, the binomial standard error is 5 to 15 points, the same size as every effect reported in the memory literature. `MIN_STACK_RECORDS = 8` is far below what a stack-specific ranking needs to be anything but noise with a confidence interval drawn around it. The ledger's design limits (never reorder phases, fall back to global history, rank by the lower bound) make it cheap to be wrong about, which is the right call. They do not make it informative. The `suspect` output is on genuinely firmer ground, because "this playbook never confirmed anything across many attempts" is a maintainer's diagnostic about the playbook rather than a claim about agent performance, and it is the one output that should be kept and advertised as such.

## the design can see swings of eight challenges and nothing smaller

The statistics here are not arguable and they are the reason the project's headline result is a null. Conditional on the discordant pairs, exact McNemar needs **at least 6 discordant pairs all in one direction** before it can reject at two-sided 0.05 at all; with 9 to 12 discordant pairs the minority side must be at most 1 or 2. At N = 26 and a realistic discordance rate of 0.30, the minimum true difference with 80% power is **0.295**; at N = 30 it is 0.275 and at N = 40 it is 0.245. **When discordance is 0.20 or below, 80% power is unreachable at any effect size**, because the expected number of discordant pairs is 5 to 8. A 20-point difference at N = 30 has 40% power under the exact test and 52% under mid-p; reaching 80% needs about 60 challenges, and a 10-point difference needs 233. Mythos observed one discordant pair in 26 and zero in 25. The design could not have detected anything, and the choice of test can decide the verdict on its own: at b = 9, c = 2 the exact p is 0.065 and mid-p is 0.039, which is why the mid-p-versus-exact choice has to be fixed before looking. Mid-p is the better-performing default and did not violate the nominal level in any of 9,595 simulated scenarios, while exact conditional and continuity-corrected asymptotic tests performed poorly throughout ([Fagerland et al. 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3716987/)). Exact McNemar at N = 30 spends only 0.021 of the nominal 0.05.

Repeats cannot rescue a small challenge set. The paired sample-size relation has a floor set by the variance of the per-challenge difference in true solve probabilities, which does not shrink with K: going from 3 to 10 repeats buys roughly 3 points of minimum detectable effect at N = 30, so **more challenges beat more repeats** ([Miller 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640)). CLT-based error bars are too narrow at small N and all non-Bayesian paired methods underperform in coverage, which argues for Wilson intervals per arm and a paired interval or Bayesian interval for the difference ([Bowyer et al., ICML 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01747)). At these sizes the intervals are wide enough to end most arguments: 0 of 26 gives a Wilson interval of 0.000 to 0.129, and 2 of 40 gives 0.014 to 0.165. The Vuln Bank separation rests on two runs per condition, for which no interval is worth printing.

Run-to-run noise is the same size as the effects being chased. Across 60,000 trajectories on SWE-bench Verified with 10 independent runs per configuration, **single-run pass@1 varied by 2.2 to 6.0 points depending on which run was picked, standard deviation stayed above 1.5 points at temperature 0, and the best-to-worst pass@k gap reached 24.9 points**; the authors conclude that gains of 2 to 3 points may be evaluation noise and recommend multiple independent runs, power analysis, and reporting pass@k and pass^k with k > 1 ([arXiv 2602.07150](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07150)). Infrastructure is a confounder rather than noise: resource limits alone moved Terminal-Bench 2 scores by **6 points (p < 0.01)**, infra errors fell from 5.8% to 0.5% with more headroom, and leaderboard gaps under 3 points "deserve skepticism" until configuration is documented and matched ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/infrastructure-noise)). At N = 30 a single challenge is 3.3 points, so if 8 of 30 challenges are coin-flips the standard deviation of one arm's score is 4.7 points on its own.

Three procedural points round this out. Outcome-dependent exclusion is a **post-randomization exclusion**, the clinical per-protocol problem, and it biases toward whichever arm's failures get dropped; the agent-evaluation analogue of intention-to-treat is that every attempted cell enters the denominator and failures count as failures. Many "infrastructure" failures are arm-dependent, since more tokens means more context overflow and rate limits, and a heavier container means more OOM kills, so the paired structure does not protect against it. Published practice is to pre-register the failure taxonomy, apply it blind to arm and outcome, and report the excluded share: DeepSWE excludes provider and grading errors from both numerator and denominator and reports the excluded fraction as 0% to 5.3% ([arXiv 2607.07946](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.07946)). Misattribution is easy: a 400-run pentest study initially recorded 39 truncated runs as model refusals when a full-log audit showed they were HTTP 529 upstream overloads ([arXiv 2605.30096](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30096)). Mythos's `classify()` is already outcome-blind and documents that choice, which is more than the published work does; what is missing is the sensitivity trio (intention-to-treat, complete-case, and worst-case bounds) and the flow table.

On blinding and agreement, the evidence is contested on average effect and consistent on the risk: a 2025 meta-epidemiological study estimated that non-blinded assessors exaggerated odds ratios by **29% (8% to 45%)**, largest for judgment-based outcomes, while MetaBLIND over 142 Cochrane meta-analyses found no average difference by blinding status. Pre-filled labels and tool suggestions anchor annotators; editing a tagger's output rather than annotating from scratch produced clear anchoring and overestimated parser performance ([Berzak et al., EMNLP 2016](https://people.csail.mit.edu/berzak/papers/D16-1239.pdf)), and in a randomised experiment with 2,784 participants, AI-favourable annotators overrelied on suggestions ([arXiv 2509.08514](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.08514v1)). `bench/labs.py` now blinds reports and seals the arm mapping, and it keeps the keyword prefill as the rater's starting pile, which should stay out of the rater's view and be measured for false negatives by rating a sample of unflagged items. Its agreement statistic is Cohen's kappa with no interval, computed over 9 to 76 ground-truth rows. At about 30 items a kappa of 0.8 cannot be distinguished from 0.6, and with skewed marginals kappa can be low while observed agreement is high, which is why positive and negative agreement indices, PABAK or Gwet's AC1 belong next to it, with a bootstrap interval ([kappa paradox review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5712640/)). Roughly 150 rated runs are needed before the interval narrows usefully.

The last procedural hazard is multiplicity and reuse. Correction formulas fix only one of the two problems: with three confirmatory comparisons, Holm requires the smallest p below 0.0167, which at N = 30 means essentially all discordant pairs favouring one arm. The other problem is not fixable by correction at all. Adaptive reuse of the same 30 challenges to develop the framework invalidates later p-values regardless of adjustment, and the coverage gate was written in response to the ungated Vuln Bank runs and scored on Vuln Bank, which is the limitation a picoCTF orchestration study lists about itself ([arXiv 2607.02873](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02873)). An audit of 41 self-improving-agent papers coded against five evaluation traps found **38 of 41 failing at least two**, with the two most-failed being fewer than five seeds and no budget-matched control; the audit is a single unreplicated preprint whose arXiv id I could not resolve, so treat the count as indicative rather than as a citation.

## prompt injection is a budget-dependent rate, never a yes or no

The field's central result is that static evaluation of injection defenses is worthless. Fourteen authors adapted gradient, RL, random-search and human-guided attacks to 12 published jailbreak and prompt-injection defenses and **bypassed all 12 with ASR above 90% for most, where the majority had originally reported near-zero**: spotlighting from about 1% to over 95%, prompt sandwiching from about 1% to over 95%, MetaSecAlign from 2% to 96%, StruQ broken in every case tested, and the Protect AI, PromptGuard and Model Armor detectors all above 90%. A human red team of over 500 participants across 40 challenges defeated every challenge. The authors' own conclusions are the citable part: "small static evals can be misleading", ASRs are not comparable across defenses, auto-raters can be reward-hacked, and "empirical evaluations cannot prove that a defense is robust" ([arXiv 2510.09023](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.09023)). Plan-then-execute designs including CaMeL and tool filtering were deliberately not attacked, because their control flow is independent of the data, and the authors note those are no cure-all and should be evaluated where control flow does depend on data. An earlier NAACL 2025 Findings paper had already broken eight indirect-injection defenses with ASR above 50% ([arXiv 2503.00061](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.00061)).

Attack success is a function of attacker budget, which is why a single ASR number means nothing. Over 945 attacker-defender battles across 21 scenarios, first-round-only ASR was **0% to 1% and rose to 7.9% to 16.8% over up to 15 adaptive rounds**; a single provenance paragraph in the prompt cut successful attacks from 110 of 270 to 70 of 270 in six scenarios ([arXiv 2607.18063](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18063)). A vendor benchmark reports the same ladder per model: one query 4.8% ± 1.7, eleven independent queries 17.6% ± 4.9, and iteration to 16 rounds **41.2% ± 5.7** for the strongest model tested ([IPI Bench](https://generalanalysis.com/blog/ipi-bench)). A generative benchmark found a filter with 0% static ASR recovering to 28% overall and **64% on "action-open" tasks** where the user delegates the action to external content, though those figures come from a search summary and should be checked against the paper before quotation ([arXiv 2606.15057](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.15057)). Framing moves everything: overt injection classes scored 0% on one model while reframing the same payload as a mandatory integrity signature or a look-alike trusted host took it **from 0% to 100%**, and a destination allow-list and a planner/reader capability split each reduced ASR to 0% ([arXiv 2608.27092](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27092)).

On oracles, the more rigorous benchmarks decide success by deterministic state checks over the environment rather than by a judge: AgentDojo explicitly does not use an LLM judge, which is why a successful injection cannot also fool its evaluator, and reports GPT-4o at 69.00% benign utility, 50.08% utility under attack and 47.69% targeted ASR over 629 security cases, with its detector defense at 7.95% ASR but **benign utility collapsing to 41.49%** ([arXiv 2406.13352](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.13352)). InjecAgent scores parsed model output with simulated tool results, which is weaker. The outcome ladder the sources support without any of them stating it as a standard is: compliant-sounding text, then an attempted tool call, then a verifiable side effect or an attacker-observed callback. Only the third should close a finding. There is **no published threshold for how many trials make an injection real** and no bug-bounty programme text requiring one; the nearest reference points are that "success in any rollout" is the stated criterion in the multi-query vendor setting, and that pass@10 of 78% coexisted with 36% success on all ten attempts in a computer-use study.

The scope boundary is settled in practice even though it is unsettled in theory. Google's AI VRP excludes direct prompt injection, jailbreaks and alignment issues, and includes indirect injection with demonstrated harm at a base reward up to $20,000 and $30,000 with multipliers, on the stated rationale that a jailbreak typically affects only the user's own session. The lethal trifecta (private data, untrusted content, an external communication channel) and Meta's Rule of Two are practitioner frameworks, not peer-reviewed work. The in-the-wild template is EchoLeak (**CVE-2025-32711**, Microsoft 365 Copilot), whose chain defeats a separate control at each step: delivery by email with no user interaction, evasion of the XPIA classifier, reference-style Markdown circumventing link redaction, an auto-fetched image as the carrier, and a CSP-allowed Teams proxy as the exfiltration path ([arXiv 2509.10540](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.10540)). A report should list each bypassed control with its own evidence.

Mythos's `ai-app` skill is ahead of most published practice on this: k = 8 interleaved trials, a matched control payload, a per-trial canary, an OAST sink, a closure band requiring n/k at or above 0.5 with control 0/k, trifecta legs named and the victim principal identified. Three things are missing and all are cheap. The rate needs an interval, because 8 of 8 is a Wilson lower bound near 0.68 and 3 of 3 is near 0.44, so a band boundary at 0.5 is inside the noise at small k. The attacker budget needs stating, and the claim needs labelling as per-attempt or Success@N, since those differ by a factor of three to eight in every source above. And nothing in the skill should ever be read as "this target is not injectable": a fixed payload pool that produces no success bounds the rate for that pool and that budget only.

## what Mythos should change, and what it cannot say

The single highest-value change is to the primary comparison. Mythos-versus-control on single-flag XBEN challenges is three bad choices at once: the benchmark is retired by its maintainer, both arms sit at 0.85 to 0.90 where there is no room for an effect, and the arms differ in skills, sub-agents, toolbox, prompt and tool access simultaneously, so the result is a comparison of bundles. Replace it with **coverage gate on against coverage gate off, within the Mythos arm, on the flagless recall targets, paired per documented vulnerability**. That design fixes every defect. It is a single factor with the gate state now recorded as a field. It has an observed effect in both directions and a large one: the gate moved Vuln Bank by about 5 of 76 entries and inverted DVGA by 6 of 21, roughly 29 points. It is the one lever in the project with no published competitor, because **no paper in this review isolates an anti-early-termination mechanism with an effect size in a security setting**, which makes a clean measurement a contribution rather than a replication. And pairing per documented vulnerability rather than per target takes N from 4 to roughly 110 to 130, which is the first time the project's sample size crosses into the range where a 20-point effect is detectable (about 56 to 60 paired units at discordance 0.3). The caveat is clustering: entries within one target share a surface map and an agent trajectory, so the analysis needs a clustered bootstrap over targets or a mixed-effects model with a target random effect, and the per-row data to do it already sits in the triage files. The cumulative-recall finding across three runs in the EthiBench protocol and that paper's own 3-run low-power caveat are the model to follow and to beat.

On verification rules, four concrete changes follow from the measurements. First, make the oracle mechanical rather than textual: `scripts/findings` should refuse to emit `validated` without an oracle identifier, a negative-control field and a replay record, so the closure state cannot be a word the model typed. The exploit skill's rule 6 already says the deterministic signal outranks the model; the schema should enforce it, because judges override environment signals about one call in ten with fewer than 8% of overrides justified. Second, separate found-once from replays: single-run reproduction rates differ from multi-trial ones by a factor of two (28.9% against 66.7% at 30 trials on CyberGym), so the validator pass should replay the stored PoC k times against a reset target and store the replay fraction. Any k is a design choice, not a published threshold, and the report should say so. Third, fix the validator's independence problem by either running it under a second `models.json` profile or recording `single_model_validation` in `findings.json`, since the 11.9-point self-approval bias follows authorship and a fresh context does not remove it. Fourth, whenever a verification or triage layer suppresses anything, publish the suppression rate next to the reduction, because the best-measured security verifier bought a drop from 98.3% to 6.3% false positives at the price of 22.25% of true positives and 77% to 85% on specific weakness classes.

On benchmark design, keep XBEN as a regression check and say so in every table. Add the measurement nobody has published: **a mutated private variant of at least one seeded app**, with renamed routes, moved parameters and reseeded bugs, run as a paired control against the public original, and report the delta as the project's contamination estimate. That single number would be the first quantitative evidence about contamination in the Juice Shop and Vuln Bank class of target, where the literature currently has only anecdote. Add a no-network condition for one sweep, since a no-cheat instruction alone cost 29% relative on one agent and infrastructure probing replaced web search under stronger prompts. Keep `flag_provenance` and print the provenance-audited rate beside the headline rate, because re-scoring on provenance cost published models 17.4 to 22.6 points. On the ground truth, adopt the dfbench practice of human-reviewing out-of-ground-truth findings instead of scoring them as false positives, and estimate the answer key's own miss rate by having a rater examine a random sample of entries no arm reported; without that, precision is a lower bound and recall is relative to an incomplete list, which is what EthiBench, dfbench and SastBench each concede about themselves. Report cost over all runs rather than over successes, since failures are the expensive ones (one plain-agent study measured $0.435 per solved task against $3.474 per failed one), and add cost-of-pass, tokens by type and wall-clock beside every rate. Report pass@1, the unbiased pass@K estimator, and pass^K, never the naive 1 minus (1 minus p) to the k.

On reporting discipline, write `bench/METHODOLOGY.md`, which the README already links and which does not exist, and put the preregistered protocol in it under a timestamped tag before the next confirmatory sweep: one primary endpoint, mid-p or exact fixed in advance, two-sided, N and K fixed, the failure taxonomy, the rater procedure, the intention-to-treat primary with the complete-case and worst-case sensitivity analyses, and Holm for the confirmatory family. Split the targets into a development set and a held-out confirmatory set, and never inspect confirmatory failures before the primary analysis; the coverage gate's history makes this non-optional. Add a bootstrap interval and a prevalence-robust index to the inter-rater statistic. State the minimum detectable effect in the paper in plain words. There is no preregistration norm in this literature at all, which means doing it is cheap differentiation as well as good practice.

On the lessons ledger, keep it and reframe it. Gate writes on an oracle-backed `validated` state only, record one attempt row per class the phase actually ran rather than one per finding written, restrict stored class and playbook strings to a closed enum so target-derived text cannot steer future engagements, and publish the `suspect` output as the ledger's product while labelling `prior` as an untested hypothesis. If the prior is ever to be claimed as a benefit, the design the literature demands is the same model, the same harness, a no-ledger control, at least five runs per arm, shuffled target order, a matched token budget, unseen targets, and paired differences with intervals. Nothing else will do, because the independent replication of the nearest published mechanism came out at -0.7 points and p = 0.23, and fell 4 to 5 points below baseline under shuffling.

The claims the literature says Mythos cannot make at its current sample sizes:

| claim | what the evidence allows | why |
|---|---|---|
| the scaffolding improves on a bare model | nothing | 26 and 25 pairs with 1 and 0 discordant; exact McNemar needs 6 in one direction, about 23 points |
| no difference, or a null result | nothing in either direction | absence of power, not evidence of absence; minimum detectable effect 0.295 at N = 26, discordance 0.30 |
| the coverage gate improves recall | a hypothesis to test on a held-out target | n = 2 per arm, no interval, ground-truth access asymmetry, and the gate was fitted to the target it was scored on |
| precision 1.00 | withdrawn, correctly | the triage schema had no `fp` row; the number was a property of the schema |
| the validator pass independently verifies a finding | a fresh-context replay on the same model | every `models.json` profile maps `strong` to one model; self-approval bias is +11.9 points and follows authorship |
| the seven-agent decomposition helps | nothing | no measurement exists; multi-agent penalties are largest in sequential tool-heavy regimes, and the capability threshold sits near 45% single-agent accuracy |
| the knowledge base improves performance | nothing, and the sign may be negative | curated skills gain +16.2 points at 2 to 3 loaded, but 4 or more gives +5.9 and comprehensive gives -2.9; removing the knowledge base improved 3 of 6 pentest frameworks |
| the lessons ledger improves later engagements | nothing | no study of cross-engagement memory in security exists; the nearest replication is -0.7 points, p = 0.23, and -4 to -5 under shuffled order |
| XBEN performance demonstrates capability | a regression check only | the maintainer retired the suite on 2026-07-07 as trained into the models |
| the scope guard prevents out-of-scope requests | it reports them after the fact | warn mode is a prompt-grade control by measurement; a severe anti-cheat prompt still left 8.5% propensity and 8 of 22 models violating |
| a target is not vulnerable to prompt injection | the rate is bounded for that payload pool and that budget | 12 defenses at near-zero static ASR went above 90% adaptively; ASR rises from 0 to 1% in one round to 41% at 16 |

What Mythos can claim, and what no competitor in this literature can, is the harness: outcome-blind infrastructure classification, flag provenance as a necessary condition for a solve, blinded double rating with a sealed arm map, a triage schema in which a false positive has somewhere to go, the unbiased pass@k estimator, Wilson intervals, a printed minimum detectable effect, and a code-level destructive denylist rather than a prompt rule. Against a field where the Agentic Benchmark Checklist found reporting limitations in all ten benchmarks it audited, where EthiBench runs 3 times and SastBench once and Doyensec uses two applications, and where not one source in this review contains a preregistered security-agent experiment, that is the publishable contribution. It is a methods paper with an honest null attached, not a capability paper.

## Conclusion

The two things this literature agrees on are that the model is now the product and the oracle is the deliverable. Scaffold effects that looked like 12 to 16 points in original reports come back as -0.7 to +9.6 against matched-model baselines, and a plain coding CLI beats most purpose-built pentest frameworks on the one unified re-run anybody has done. Meanwhile the verification numbers have barely moved toward comfort: 44% of marker-validated PoCs were fake, a lying target took grounded SQL-injection proof from 44% to 0%, a judge approves its own errors 11.9 points more often and overrides a correct environment signal one time in ten, and ten unanimous reviewers endorsed a vulnerability that did not exist. A framework whose value proposition is "the methodology and the proof standard, with your model" is therefore pointed in the right direction and happens to be pointed at the part of the problem that is not saturating. A framework whose value proposition is "our scaffold solves more challenges" is pointed at a number that retired in July 2026.

The uncomfortable implication is for the project's own positioning rather than its code. The honest null on XBEN and the withdrawn Vuln Bank separation are not setbacks to be measured away; they are the expected outcome, and spending the next sweep trying to make the scaffold separate from a bare model on a saturated suite would be the same mistake with better instrumentation. The measurements that would actually be new are small, specific and within reach: a public-versus-mutated-variant delta for a seeded web app, which would be the field's first contamination number for that class of target; an isolated coverage-gate effect per documented vulnerability, which would be the field's first anti-early-termination effect size in security; and a replay-rate distribution for stored web PoCs, which nobody has published at all. Each is a single-factor experiment with a plausible effect size above the 25-point floor the design can see, and each sits in a hole the literature openly admits to. The ledger, the seven agents and the playbook corpus should be carried as untested design decisions with their costs disclosed, because that is what they are, and because a project that says so in its own README has already demonstrated the only thing this field is actually short of.
