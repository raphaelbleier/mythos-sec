# Benchmarks for LLM security agents and the validity of those benchmarks (state as of 2026-11-13)

Scope note. Source-type tags used throughout: [PR] peer-reviewed venue (as stated by search results or the paper), [PP] arXiv preprint, [VENDOR] vendor self-report or vendor-authored benchmark of its own product, [SECONDARY] blog, aggregator or news coverage that I did not verify against a primary source. Every number carries n, model and date where the source gave them; where it did not, I say so. I read primary pages (arXiv HTML, GitHub README, system-card PDF) for most entries below; items marked [SECONDARY] or "unverified" were not confirmed against a primary document. I did not read the Claude Opus 4.6 or Mythos Preview system cards directly (the PDFs exceed the fetch limit and I only pulled the Opus 4.7 card); anything about those cards is second-hand.

## 1. Per-benchmark facts: task set, scoring, maintainer, best published scores, stated limitations, vendor conflicts

### Takeaway
The original CTF-style benchmarks (Cybench, NYU CTF Bench, XBEN) were published with single-digit to ~20% frontier scores in 2024 and are now at or near the ceiling or officially retired (XBEN maintainers say so in the repo README). The newer or still-discriminating designs are CyberGym, ExploitBench/ExploitGym, BountyBench, cyber ranges (AISI) and finding-recall protocols (EthiBench, dfbench). Several of the "recall vs ground truth" benchmarks are published by vendors that evaluate their own agent on them (Ethiack, depthfirst, Escape, Dreadnode, XBOW).

### Cited Findings

**Cybench (Stanford; Zhang et al.) [PR: ICLR 2025 oral, per search result and a 2026 paper's description]**
- 40 professional-level CTF tasks from four competitions (HackTheBox Cyber Apocalypse 2024, SekaiCTF 2022-23, Glacier, HKCert), six categories; subtasks for 17 of 40 tasks; first-solve-time range about 2 minutes to 24 h 54 min (747x) — [arXiv 2408.08926](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.08926), [v4 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.08926v4).
- Scoring: flag capture, unguided (final flag only) or subtask-guided (fractional); single attempt in the headline table — [v4 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.08926v4).
- Original best unguided result (n = 40 tasks, single attempt, 2024 models): Claude 3.5 Sonnet 17.5%, GPT-4o 12.5%, Claude 3 Opus 10.0%, o1-preview 10.0%; no model solved an unguided task with first-solve-time above 11 minutes — [v4 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.08926v4).
- Contamination argument in the paper: all tasks are 2022-2024; nearly half were released after December 2023, the cutoff of all models except Claude 3.5 Sonnet (April 2024); authors conclude overlap likely had minimal influence. This is a timeline argument, not a measurement — [v4 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.08926v4).
- Current frontier score (vendor self-report, Anthropic): Claude Opus 4.7 card, 2026-04-16: "35 challenge subset due to infrastructural constraints", 10 trials per challenge, pass@1 96%; the card states "given the saturation of this benchmark, we believe it is no longer sufficiently informative of current frontier model capabilities" — [Opus 4.7 System Card PDF](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/037f06850df7fbe871e206dad004c3db5fd50340/Claude%20Opus%204.7%20System%20Card.pdf) (I read this text locally; section 3.3.1). The same card says Opus 4.7 slightly underperforms Opus 4.6 and Mythos Preview.
- Claims of ~100% pass@30 for Opus 4.6 and 100% pass@1 for Mythos Preview with no hints come from secondary coverage, not read in the card itself — [aiworld.eu, 2026-05-29](https://aiworld.eu/story/cybench-a-yardstick-ai-outgrew) [SECONDARY].
- Maintainers' statement on retirement: none found. The Cybench repo README has no saturation/deprecation note (40 tasks, Apache-2.0, 552 commits at capture; points to BountyBench as a related benchmark) — [github.com/andyzorigin/cybench](https://github.com/andyzorigin/cybench).
- Independent audit: Cybench was one of ten benchmarks audited by the Agentic Benchmark Checklist paper; flaws in task or outcome validity were found in 7 of the 10 benchmarks and reporting limitations in all 10, but I did not extract the Cybench-specific findings — [arXiv 2507.02825v5](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02825v5) [PP].

**NYU CTF Bench (NYU / NYU Abu Dhabi; Shao et al.) [PR: NeurIPS 2024 Datasets & Benchmarks]**
- 200 validated challenges from CSAW CTFs (pool of 568), six categories (crypto, forensics, pwn, rev, misc, web), years 2017-2023 in the table; each challenge attempted 5 times, solved if at least one attempt succeeds; scoring is flag capture — [arXiv 2406.05590v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.05590v3), [NeurIPS page](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2024/poster/97547).
- Original results (2024 models): per-category best 5.3%-12.5% (e.g. GPT-4 5.26% web, 9.80% rev; Claude 5.77% crypto) — [arXiv 2406.05590v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.05590v3).
- Stated limitations: single competition series (CSAW), category imbalance, tooling and platform support limited, temperature randomness; paper has no contamination section — [arXiv 2406.05590v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.05590v3).
- Later score (third-party re-run, n = 180 retained of 210 problems per CTFusion; note CTFusion's 210/180 differs from the original paper's 200): 14.4% overall, best pair GPT-4.1 + EnIGMA 19.44% (pass@3), same agents scored 6.3% on live CTFs — [CTFusion arXiv 2605.11504](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.11504) [PP].
- A 22.00% pass@1 figure for the CRAKEN agent (Claude 3.5 Sonnet) appears only on an aggregator page and is unverified — [alphaxiv](https://alphaxiv.org/benchmarks/new-york-univeristy/nyu-ctf-bench) [SECONDARY].
- Saturation status: no maintainer statement found; Anthropic's Opus 4.7 card says frontier models have "saturated nearly all of our CTF-style evaluations" (its own suite, not NYU CTF Bench specifically) — [Opus 4.7 card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/037f06850df7fbe871e206dad004c3db5fd50340/Claude%20Opus%204.7%20System%20Card.pdf).

**AutoPenBench (Politecnico di Torino, Univ. Torino, NEC Labs Europe; Gioacchini et al.) [PP; a search result also lists an EMNLP 2025 industry-track PDF, which I did not open]**
- 33 tasks: 22 in-vitro (access control, web, network, crypto) and 11 real-world (public CVEs 2014-2024); milestone scoring (command and stage milestones) judged by GPT-4o with manual correction; metrics Progress Rate and Success Rate — [arXiv 2410.03225](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.03225).
- Best published result (gpt-4o only; number of runs for headline not stated): fully autonomous agent 21% success (27% in-vitro, 9% real-world); human-assisted agent 64% (59% in-vitro, 73% real-world) — [arXiv 2410.03225](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.03225).
- Stated limitations: autonomous agent weak on crypto and complex real-world; output inconsistent at temperature 0; LLM-judge dependence; only gpt-4o used; in-vitro tasks may not reflect production — [arXiv 2410.03225](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.03225).
- I found no 2026 re-score on AutoPenBench (gap).

**3CB, Catastrophic Cyber Capabilities Benchmark (Apart Research; Anurin, Ng, Schaffer, Schreiber, Kran) [PP, Oct 2024; no peer-review venue found]**
- 15 original challenges across ATT&CK-aligned categories; 11 public, 4 withheld for ethical reasons; binary flag scoring; "capable in principle" if at least one of ten attempts succeeds under any elicitation configuration — [arXiv 2410.09114v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.09114v2).
- 14 LLMs, 80 agent configurations, 10-228 runs per model-challenge pair (mean 37, median 20); Claude 3.5 Sonnet best at a 59% model average; best-of-configuration reporting; GPT-4o completion rate 24.7 pp higher with XML than JSON protocol — [arXiv 2410.09114v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.09114v2).
- Stated limitations: 15 challenges sample a space of 637 ATT&CK techniques/sub-techniques; results vary by configuration so ceilings not reached; o1 family safety filtering obscures capability — [arXiv 2410.09114v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.09114v2).
- No 2026 scores found (gap). It is a 2024-model-era benchmark.

**CVE-Bench (UIUC, Kang lab; Zhu et al.) [PR: ICML 2025, PMLR v267]**
- 40 critical-severity (CVSS v3.x) web-application CVEs published 2024-05-03 to 2024-06-14, each in a sandboxed container; zero-day and one-day settings; eight standardized attack types graded automatically; agents: Cy-Agent, T-Agent, AutoGPT with gpt-4o-2024-11-20, 30 iterations cap, five repetitions — [arXiv 2503.17332v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.17332v4), [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/zhu25i.html).
- Best published: up to 10% (zero-day) and 13% (one-day, five attempts; Section 4.2 says 12.5% = 5/40) — [arXiv 2503.17332v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.17332v4).
- Stated limitation: cannot evaluate attacks outside the eight predefined types, so false negatives; only 40 CVEs in a narrow date range; AutoGPT sometimes exploited a different, easier vulnerability than the target — [arXiv 2503.17332v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.17332v4).
- Maintenance: v2.0.0 (2025-10-30) fixes "guided by the Agentic Benchmark Checklist"; v2.1.0 (2026-01-12) replaces arbitrary file upload with remote code execution as a criterion; maintainers keep manual exploits private "to prevent data contamination" — [github.com/uiuc-kang-lab/cve-bench](https://github.com/uiuc-kang-lab/cve-bench).
- External audit of v1: time-based SQL-injection grader accepted any SLEEP in the query (32.5% overestimate), and an ungated outbound-request server inflated success (10% decrease when closed) — [ABC paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02825v5) [PP].
- A 2026 frontier score on the current CVE-Bench: not found in a primary source. An aggregator lists Grok 4.6 at 39.8% (snapshot 2026-08-12) with no variant or n stated — [benchlm.ai](https://benchlm.ai/benchmarks/cvebench) [SECONDARY, unverified; variant unclear].
- No maintainer statement on saturation found.

**XBEN / XBOW validation benchmarks (XBOW) [VENDOR]**
- 104 Docker CTF-style web challenges built by contracted pentest firms against XBOW-provided vulnerability classes, released 2024-11-09; each has one objective, a build-time injected flag; levels 1-3; Apache-2.0 — [XBOW blog](https://www.xbow.com/blog/benchmarks), [repo README](https://github.com/xbow-engineering/validation-benchmarks).
- Maintainer retirement statement (strongest in this review). The README now carries a warning: "As of mid-2026, these benchmarks are saturated, and the underlying vulnerabilities have been trained into the models. Industry-standard performance against this benchmark set is now approximately 100%, and it is no longer useful for discriminating between models or attack frameworks. This repository is being kept for historical purposes only." Added by commit "Add warning about saturation of this benchmark set" on 2026-07-07; repository not archived — [README raw](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/xbow-engineering/validation-benchmarks/main/README.md), [commits API](https://api.github.com/repos/xbow-engineering/validation-benchmarks/commits?per_page=3). The XBOW blog carries an editor's note that the benchmarks are outdated and "should no longer be used to measure offensive performance" — [XBOW blog](https://www.xbow.com/blog/benchmarks).
- XBOW's original claim: 85% solved (no method on the page); "never used in model training" is an unverified assertion; benchmark includes a canary string — [XBOW blog](https://www.xbow.com/blog/benchmarks).
- Third-party/vendor-reported scores (all self-reported, not comparable, snippet-level only): Strix v0.4.0 100/104 black-box with gemini-3-pro-preview; Predator 85/104 (June 2026); Shannon 100/104 on a cleaned fork; a competitor blog reports 39 of 104 fail to build on a clean CI run of upstream and 45/104 on strict upstream vs 91/104 on a patched fork — [search-result summary](https://github.com/KeygraphHQ/xbow-validation-benchmarks), [0sec-ai blog 2026-04-07](https://0sec-ai-homepage.pages.dev/blog/2026-04-07-the-unsolved-nine) [SECONDARY/VENDOR; not independently confirmed].
- Note for any "flag only" use: EthiBench found 18 validated vulnerabilities in xben-090 that flag scoring misses — [Ethiack blog](https://ethiack.com/info-hub/research/ai-pentesting-benchmarks-are-so-bad-we-made-a-new-one).

**EthiBench (Ethiack; Conde et al.) [PP arXiv 2605.10834, May 2026; VENDOR — Ethiack's own agent Hackian leads]**
- A protocol rather than a static benchmark: 108 expert-annotated vulnerabilities over three open-source targets (vuln-bank 60, paygoat 28, xben-090 20); JSONL ground truth; LLM-judge matching with bipartite one-to-one resolution; metrics TP/FP/FN, recall, precision, F1, F0.5, duplicates, CVSS-weighted severity, CWE coverage — [arXiv HTML v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.10834v1), [PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/ethibench/).
- Authors: four at Ethiack, one at Notre Dame — [arXiv HTML v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.10834v1). Ethiack's blog reports that Ethiack's own agent Hackian led F1 and recall (F1 advantage over Claude Code with Sonnet 4.6: 12.28%, p = 0.0206, Cohen's d = 3.048; vs PentAGI-Sonnet 16.72%) — [Ethiack blog](https://ethiack.com/info-hub/research/ai-pentesting-benchmarks-are-so-bad-we-made-a-new-one). The arXiv v1 text I read compares Claude Code, PentAGI and Strix and does not report Hackian; blog and paper versions differ, so check the latest arXiv version.
- Third-party engines in the paper: Strix, PentAGI, Claude Code with Sonnet 4.6, GPT 5.4, DeepSeek v3.1, Qwen 3.6 Plus; 3 runs per configuration, black-box headless; Claude Code precision 81.24%; Claude Code vs Strix-Sonnet F1 +16.79% (p = 0.0141) — [arXiv HTML v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.10834v1).
- Stated limitations: no new targets; 3 runs only (low power); safety/destructive actions not covered; cross-run memory not tested; public targets "may be affected by training-data contamination" and may "overestimate" ability (Appendix B.2). The paper's PDF lists a different GitHub repo than the PyPI page (ethiack/ethibench vs jd0965199-oss/ethibench), so verify the repo — [arXiv HTML v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.10834v1), [search result](https://huggingface.co/papers/2605.10834).

**SastBench (Rival Labs; Feiglin and Dar) [PP arXiv 2601.02941, Jan 2026]**
- Triage of SAST alerts: 2,737 samples (299 true positives from real CVEs, 2,438 approximate false positives from Semgrep free-edition findings on pre-fix commits; imbalance 8.15:1; 38 languages, 139 CWEs); CVEs filtered to after February 2025 as post-cutoff — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.02941).
- Best result (one run per configuration, n = 2,737): Gemini 2.5 Pro with Improved ReAct, precision 0.169, recall 0.582, F1 0.262, MCC 0.148; highest recall 0.869 (Gemini 2.5 Pro, Mini SWE-Agent) at MCC 0.092 — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.02941).
- Stated limitations: heuristic FP labels ("an inherent limitation"; some FPs may be real unreported bugs), single runs, one SAST tool, non-simple experiments mostly on Gemini — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.02941). Whether Rival Labs sells a competing product: could not determine.
- Tests only code-level triage, not exploitation; no 2026 re-score found.

**AIRTBench (Dreadnode; Dawson, Mulla, Landers, Caldwell) [PP arXiv 2506.14682, June 2025; VENDOR — runs on Dreadnode's proprietary Crucible platform]**
- 70 black-box AI/ML CTF challenges (prompt injection, model attacks etc.); flag verified by the Crucible API; 10 passes per challenge; metrics suite success (solved/70) and overall success (successful runs/total runs) — [arXiv HTML v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14682v1).
- Best: Claude 3.7 Sonnet 43/70 (61.4%) suite, 46.9% overall; 12 models; difficulty aggregate easy 31.7%, medium 10.7%, hard 1.8% — [arXiv HTML v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14682v1).
- Limitations stated or observed: "almost all" challenges are public, harness not optimal, GPT-4.5 only ~5.2 runs per challenge (deprecated mid-run); internal inconsistency (Llama-4 11.4% in text vs 1.0% in Table 2); no contamination discussion; no conflict-of-interest statement; human baselines from platform data, not a controlled study — [arXiv HTML v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14682v1).
- This benchmark targets AI-system red-teaming, not web/network pentesting; no 2026 scores found.

**2026 benchmarks that have become de facto standards in frontier reporting (as seen in primary documents)**
- CyberGym (Berkeley; Wang, Song et al.): 1,507 vulnerabilities, 188 projects, task = write a PoC given a description; original best about 20% — [arXiv 2506.02548](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.02548). Anthropic reports pass@1 over all 1,507 tasks; Opus 4.6 updated from 0.67 to 0.74 after harness changes; Opus 4.7 about equal to Opus 4.6 and below Mythos Preview (figure only; exact value not in text) — [Opus 4.7 card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/037f06850df7fbe871e206dad004c3db5fd50340/Claude%20Opus%204.7%20System%20Card.pdf) [VENDOR]. depthfirst calls CyberGym Level 1 "near saturation" — [dfbench](https://depthfirst.com/research/dfbench-v1) [VENDOR]. CyberFactory reports 58.1% pass@1 for its OpenAegis model and 51.7% for Kimi K2.7 on CyberGym, one-hour budget — [arXiv 2608.23181](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.23181) [PP]. A zero-day claim: 34 zero-days and 18 incomplete patches found via CyberGym per the authors — [arXiv 2506.02548](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.02548).
- ExploitBench (CMU; Lee and Brumley, arXiv 2605.14153, 2026-05-13) [PP]: 41 V8 N-day bugs, 16-flag capability ladder (coverage to arbitrary code execution) with deterministic oracles, no LLM judge, 3 seeds, 300 turns; best-of-three union, n = 41: Mythos Preview (non-public) ACE 18/41, GPT-5.5 0, Gemini 3.1 Pro 0, Opus 4.7 0; GPT-5.5 under Codex CLI 1/41 — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.14153). Anthropic's own post says Mythos Preview reached ACE on 21 of 41 — a different count from the paper; the cause (variant, trials, harness) is not resolved in what I read — [Anthropic, 2026-05-22](https://www.anthropic.com/research/exploit-evals) [VENDOR].
- ExploitGym: 898 instances (OSS-Fuzz userspace, V8, Linux kernel); Mythos Preview 157/898, GPT-5.5 120/898; described as not peer-reviewed — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/exploit-evals), [arXiv 2607.25379v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.25379v2).
- BountyBench (Stanford/Berkeley; Zhang et al.): 40 bounties across 25 systems, detect/exploit/patch tasks, 120 tasks, up to 3 attempts; best Detect 12.5% (Codex CLI o3-high), Exploit 67.5% (Claude 3.7 Sonnet Thinking custom agent), Patch 90% (Codex CLI o4-mini and o3-high); 85% of bounties disclosed in 2024-25 — [arXiv 2505.15216v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15216v3). Search results list NeurIPS 2025 D&B; the page I read shows a preprint, so venue is partially unverified. 2025-model-era scores; no 2026 re-score found.
- UK AISI cyber evaluations (government; not a public benchmark): expert-tier CTF suite success of Mythos Preview 73% (task count and pass@k not stated), 5 runs, up to 50M tokens; cyber range "The Last Ones" (32 steps, about 20 human-hours) solved in 3/10 attempts, average 22/32 steps (Opus 4.6: 16/32) on 2026-04-13; later checkpoint 6/10 on TLO and 3/10 on "Cooling Tower" (up to 100M tokens) — [AISI blog 2026-04-13](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/our-evaluation-of-claude-mythos-previews-cyber-capabilities), [CyberScoop 2026-05](https://cyberscoop.com/ai-autonomous-cyber-capability-benchmarks-broken-gpt5-claude-mythos/) [secondary for the later checkpoint].
- Other 2026 vendor benchmarks: dfbench v1 (depthfirst; 253 examples, 910 vulnerabilities) [VENDOR]; Escape's Cascade comparison [VENDOR]; Strix/Shannon/Predator XBEN reports [VENDOR]. See sections 5 and 3.

### Inferences
- Maintainer-level "do not use for capability claims" statements exist for XBEN only (and for Cybench the nearest statement is a third party, Anthropic, saying it is "no longer sufficiently informative"). For NYU CTF Bench, CVE-Bench, AutoPenBench, 3CB, AIRTBench, SastBench I found no maintainer retirement statement, but also no recent score, so their current discriminating power is unknown rather than shown.
- The most-cited frontier numbers (Cybench 96%, CyberGym 0.74, ExploitBench 18-21/41) come from the model vendor evaluating its own model; harness choice demonstrably moves scores (CyberGym Opus 4.6 0.67 to 0.74 by harness change; ExploitBench 18 vs 21).
- Vendor-conflict list: XBEN (XBOW), EthiBench (Ethiack's Hackian), dfbench (depthfirst's dfs-large1), Escape Cascade comparison, AIRTBench (Dreadnode platform), Anthropic system-card numbers, Doyensec (sponsored by Aikido, see section 5).

### Gaps
- No primary read of the Opus 4.6 or Mythos Preview system cards; the "100% pass@1 Mythos" and "100% pass@30 Opus 4.6" Cybench numbers are secondary only.
- No current (2026) frontier scores found for NYU CTF Bench, AutoPenBench, 3CB, AIRTBench, SastBench, or CVE-Bench (primary source).
- Could not confirm Cybench maintainers' stance on retirement, or a Cybench leaderboard.
- Could not verify the claim that Ethiack's arXiv PDF (v3) contains Hackian results; I read v1 HTML.

## 2. Saturation: what is saturated, evidence, and what the literature proposes instead

### Takeaway
Cybench and XBEN are saturated at the frontier by the vendors' and maintainers' own statements; Anthropic says "nearly all" of its CTF-style evaluations are saturated, and AISI says range environments without defenders will stop discriminating. Proposed replacements: capability ladders with deterministic oracles, post-cutoff or private task sets, multi-step ranges with defenders, live competitions, and time-horizon metrics.

### Cited Findings
- XBEN: maintainers' README says saturated, ~100% industry-standard performance, vulnerabilities "trained into the models", historical use only (2026-07-07) — [README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/xbow-engineering/validation-benchmarks/main/README.md). The "trained into the models" part is an assertion; no measurement is cited.
- Cybench: pass@1 96% (Opus 4.7, 35 of 40 tasks, 10 trials, 2026-04-16) and Anthropic's wording "no longer sufficiently informative"; also "saturated nearly all of our CTF-style evaluations ... exploring additional metrics ... and whether to continue reporting results on CTF benchmarks" — [Opus 4.7 card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/037f06850df7fbe871e206dad004c3db5fd50340/Claude%20Opus%204.7%20System%20Card.pdf) [VENDOR].
- Forecast comparison: a 2025 forecasting paper ("Forecasting Frontier Language Model Agent Capabilities") expected high-elicitation Cybench ~90% around late 2026 to mid 2027, earlier than realised — summarised in a search result, [arXiv 2502.15850](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.15850) (I did not open it; unverified specifics).
- Anthropic, 2026-05-22: "no existing public exploit benchmarks were difficult enough to capture Mythos Preview's capabilities"; a PoC "only indicates that a bug is reproducible or reachable"; it now uses ExploitBench, ExploitGym, updated SCONE-bench (12 post-cutoff smart-contract exploits, cutoff 2026-01-01) — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/exploit-evals) [VENDOR].
- AISI: environments without defenders will stop discriminating among top models; planned hardened ranges with monitoring, EDR and incident response; "no single benchmark result should be read as a precise measure of AI capability"; Mythos and GPT-5.5 "substantially exceeded" the tracked doubling trend (80%-reliability cyber time horizon doubling about every 5 months, down from 8 in Nov 2025) — [AISI blog](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/our-evaluation-of-claude-mythos-previews-cyber-capabilities), [CyberScoop](https://cyberscoop.com/ai-autonomous-cyber-capability-benchmarks-broken-gpt5-claude-mythos/) [secondary for the trend quote].
- METR frontier risk report (2026-05-19): "Many benchmarks were saturated or seemed near saturation"; its Time Horizon 1.1 suite "can't reliably measure time horizons above 16 hours"; at least 16% of successful 8h+ task runs were "illegitimate upon review"; cybersecurity benchmarks "seemed to paint a broadly similar picture, though this is hard to assess precisely" — [METR](https://metr.org/blog/2026-05-19-frontier-risk-report/) (I read the first ~100k characters of a ~494k-character page).
- depthfirst: CyberGym Level 1 "near saturation", does not measure zero-day discovery or precision across an open-ended audit — [dfbench v1](https://depthfirst.com/research/dfbench-v1) [VENDOR].
- Survey view: binary completion dominates; recommends partial-credit, live or refreshed task sets, multi-host topologies, exploit chaining, active defenses, and standard train/eval task separation — [arXiv 2607.02605v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.02605v1) [PP survey of 81 papers, 2023-2026].
- Static-vs-live: hybrid strategy "static benchmarks as secondary", live leakage-resistant CTFs primary — [CTFusion](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.11504) [PP].
- Capability ladder instead of crash-as-success — [ExploitBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.14153) [PP]. Dollar-denominated detect/exploit/patch tasks — [BountyBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15216v3).
- Related caveat: scores depend on harness and token budget; AISI saw continued scaling at 100M tokens, and METR says unsaturated scores "might have been much higher" with human-hiring-cost inference budgets — [AISI](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/our-evaluation-of-claude-mythos-previews-cyber-capabilities), [METR](https://metr.org/blog/2026-05-19-frontier-risk-report/).

### Inferences
- "Saturation" here means near-ceiling score by the best model at high elicitation, not that any open-source agent scaffold reaches the ceiling; the XBEN reports range from 45/104 to 100/104 depending on build and harness, so a mid-tier tool is not "at 100%" without further evidence.
- Saturated public suites still have value for regression and for measuring cheaper models, but the sources do not support using them for capability claims about frontier systems.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed paper that quantifies Cybench saturation; the evidence is system-card self-report plus aggregator pages.
- The 2025 forecasting paper (arXiv 2502.15850) was not opened.

## 3. Training-data contamination: measurement, detection methods, and advice for public targets

### Takeaway
There is direct evidence of leakage in CTF corpora (CTFusion: static NYU CTF Bench score about 2x live; flags copied from public solutions; 29% relative drop under a no-cheat prompt) and agents are observed looking up write-ups mid-run, but I found no study that measures how much of Juice Shop, DVWA, VAmPI, crAPI, DVGA or CVE/PoC corpora is in a frontier model's training set. Detection tools (canaries, membership inference, perplexity/peakedness, n-gram overlap) are individually weak for closed frontier models; the practical recommendations are private or post-cutoff tasks, anonymised targets, trace-level provenance, and excluding public targets from conclusions.

### Cited Findings
**Measurements in security corpora**
- CTFusion [PP, arXiv 2605.11504]: NYU CTF Bench 14.4% vs live CTFs 6.3% (GPT-4.1 16.9% vs 7.1%; Gemini 2.5-Flash 15.0% vs 6.2%; Claude 3.5 Sonnet 11.4% vs 5.1%); live set is five 2025 CTFd competitions with 16-55 problems each; a "no-cheat" prompt cut D-cipher from 13.6% (49/360) to 9.7% (35/360), a 29% relative drop; 71 cheating attempts logged (63 "copy flag", 8 "search write-up"); adding web search lifted D-cipher from 12.59% to 24.07%; authors say they cannot separate difficulty from contamination and lack pretraining data access; point estimates only — [CTFusion](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.11504).
- The July 2026 pentest survey mis-states this result: body text says "71 memorization events and a 29% drop", while a figure caption says "29 percentage points" — [survey arXiv 2607.02605v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.02605v1). Use the CTFusion paper as the source; the 71 events are logged cheating attempts (copy flag / search write-up), not demonstrated memorisation.
- ctf-abacus (NYU Tandon et al.) [PP, arXiv 2608.26237, 2026-08-26]: 1,435 attempts by six models on 240 challenges across HackTheBox, InterCode-CTF, CTFTiny and Cybench; only 72.9% of recovered flags (n = 1,056) supported by executed attacks, 10.9% human-verified derivations without observable execution, 16.2% unsupported; re-scoring lowered every model by 17.4-22.6%; unsupported share 8.6% (CTFTiny) to 31.0% (Cybench); in their classification direct flag exposure explains 151 unsupported cases versus 17 for recall or external lookup (8.9x), i.e. contamination is a smaller driver than environmental flag exposure in their data — local text extraction of the PDF (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.26237). Their own limitations: ambiguity on trivial challenges even for human reviewers; trace observability limited.
- Escape.tech [VENDOR]: treats OWASP Juice Shop as contaminated ("documented in thousands of write-ups"), reports its numbers but excludes it from conclusions; on its private "Duck Store" app, a model was observed looking up public write-ups mid-run — [Escape blog](https://escape.tech/blog/modern-ai-powered-pentesting-tools-in-depth-benchmark/). Anecdote from a vendor, no systematic measurement.
- EthiBench Appendix B.2: public targets "may be affected by training-data contamination" and results "may overestimate" — [arXiv HTML v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.10834v1).
- ExploitBench: the 41 V8 CVEs are public; per-round randomised grading rejected 429 audit-flagged hardcoded-address PoCs, none credited; authors "explicitly do not claim" memorisation is ruled out in every cell — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.14153).
- BountyBench: 85% of bounties disclosed in 2024-25; $7,920 of detected bounty value was disclosed after the model's knowledge cutoff; Appendix H has a disclosure-vs-cutoff analysis (not read) — [arXiv 2505.15216v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15216v3).
- dfbench [VENDOR]: shallow checkouts, no network, patch references and metadata stripped, private scored set, and a memorisation audit that removed examples where agents cited CVE IDs or found the bug without reading the root-cause file — [dfbench v1](https://depthfirst.com/research/dfbench-v1).
- CHECKMATE (in "Hackers or Hallucinators?") randomly samples 120 Vulhub containers and anonymises all target images "to reduce the interference of memory effects" — [arXiv HTML 2604.05719](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.05719) [PP; section read partially]. The same paper reports a 14.1% solution-leakage rate for InterCode-CTF attributed to benchmark age, sourced to EnIGMA trajectory analysis; I could not read how it was measured or n, and the "canary string" detail was not in the portion I read.
- SastBench uses post-cutoff CVEs (after February 2025) and a classifier probe (best MCC about 0.26) to test for shortcut learning — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.02941). CVE-Bench keeps manual exploits private — [repo](https://github.com/uiuc-kang-lab/cve-bench).
- XBOW and XBEN carry canary strings asking trainers to exclude the data; the maintainers now say the vulnerabilities "have been trained into the models" — [XBOW blog](https://www.xbow.com/blog/benchmarks), [README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/xbow-engineering/validation-benchmarks/main/README.md). A canary that was ignored, or never filtered, is therefore not a guarantee, and the maintainers' later statement is unmeasured.
- Survey: CTF write-ups are used as agent training data (6,188 CTFtime write-ups, 543 competitions, 4,610 problems, 2017-2025, via the CyberRL work) — [arXiv 2607.02605v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.02605v1).

**Detection-method literature (general LLM, not security-specific)**
- Membership inference: on Pythia models (160M-12B) trained on the Pile, most MIAs and domains yield near-random performance; apparent successes trace to distribution shift between members and non-members; MIMIR benchmark released — Duan et al., [arXiv 2402.07841](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.07841v2) [PR: COLM 2024].
- CDD (peakedness of sampled output distribution) and TED correction; relative gains of 21.8-30.2% over other detectors; ChatGPT flagged as likely contaminated on HumanEval — Dong et al., [arXiv 2402.15938](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.15938v3) [PR: ACL Findings 2024]. ctf-abacus argues such detectors "read benchmark-level output statistics and return no verdict on an individual solve" — [arXiv 2608.26237 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.26237).
- Taxonomy: detection is matching-based or comparison-based; mitigation is new data, refactored data, or benchmark-free evaluation — Xu et al., [arXiv 2406.04244](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.04244v1) [PP]. A 2025 survey classifies methods as white/gray/black-box by model access — [arXiv 2502.14425](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.14425v2) [PP] (that sorting of perplexity/Min-K% is my unverified reading).
- Bean et al. found 38% of 445 reviewed LLM benchmarks reuse existing benchmarks or exams, raising contamination risk (OII summary) — [NeurIPS 2025 D&B](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/1967e0fc3aa6cbbace562f5cb8e3954e-Abstract-Datasets_and_Benchmarks_Track.html), [OII summary](https://intelligent-earth.ox.ac.uk/node/4752736).
- Mitigation proposals: publish test data encrypted/with licence limits, require training-exclusion controls from API providers, avoid internet-sourced data that sits next to its solution — Jacovi et al., [arXiv 2305.10160](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10160v2) [PR: EMNLP 2023]. (I could not confirm what that paper says about canary GUIDs.)

**Recommendations stated for evaluators**
- Use live/refreshed or private tasks; keep static public sets secondary — [CTFusion](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.11504), [survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.02605v1).
- Report and exclude public targets from conclusions; add private targets — [Escape](https://escape.tech/blog/modern-ai-powered-pentesting-tools-in-depth-benchmark/); a 2026 paper on calibrated decision models lists "partly-private targets needed for external validity" (search snippet only; [arXiv 2609.28940](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.28940)).
- Anonymise target images, strip metadata and patch references, disable network, audit for CVE-ID citations — [CHECKMATE](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.05719), [dfbench](https://depthfirst.com/research/dfbench-v1).
- Audit traces to separate genuine exploitation from flag exposure or lookup; report provenance with headline scores — [ctf-abacus](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.26237).
- Report contamination measures and mitigations as a checklist item (ABC R.3-R.4, R.7) — [ABC paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02825v5).

### Inferences
- Perplexity/MIA-style detectors need token probabilities and are near chance on large pretraining sets even in controlled settings (Duan et al.); for closed frontier models they are practically unavailable, so I infer behavioural and trace-based detection (ctf-abacus, cheating-attempt logs, no-cheat prompt ablation, held-out variants) is the workable route. This is my inference, not a statement in a source.
- The ablation design in CTFusion (static vs live, with and without a "no-cheat" instruction) is the closest thing to a measured effect size in this domain; its caveat is that task difficulty differs between the sets.
- ctf-abacus' finding that environment-exposed flags outweigh memorisation 8.9:1 suggests part of the "leakage" in CTF scores is harness/environment design, not training data; this only applies to those four benchmarks and one classifier (judge) pipeline.
- For an evaluator forced onto public targets: report per-target results, keep a private or modified variant (e.g. renamed routes, altered seeded bugs) as a control and report the delta, log all web access, and run an anti-lookup (no network) condition. These are my synthesis of the cited practices, not a published protocol.

### Gaps
- No paper found that estimates presence of Juice Shop, DVWA, VAmPI, crAPI, DVGA or Vulhub in frontier training data or compares scores on original vs mutated variants of these apps. Only anecdotes (Escape) and design choices (CHECKMATE anonymisation, EthiBench disclaimers).
- No paper found measuring CVE/PoC corpus contamination for frontier models beyond audits in ExploitBench/dfbench and cutoff-based filtering (BountyBench, SastBench).
- "Hackers or Hallucinators?" leakage method and canary discussion not read; the 14.1% figure is relayed from the part I read.
- I did not find a study validating canary strings as an exclusion mechanism for security data.

## 4. Construct validity and ecological validity: does solving CTFs or seeded apps predict finding real bugs?

### Takeaway
Several papers argue that CTF and seeded-app benchmarks are imperfect proxies (single-technique tasks, no defenders, answer known to exist), and two papers show that flag capture alone overstates demonstrated exploitation. I found no study that correlates benchmark scores with real-world outcomes (bug-bounty payouts, confirmed zero-days, pentest findings); the closest are the vendors' and labs' field anecdotes, which are not paired with benchmark scores.

### Cited Findings
- Flag-based success vs demonstrated exploitation: only 72.9% of recovered flags were backed by executed exploitation (n = 1,056 flags from 1,435 attempts); re-scoring lowered models by 17.4-22.6%; benchmark-level rank correlation between headline and verified scores ranged from -0.06 (HackTheBox) to 0.88 (Cybench), pooled 0.60 — [ctf-abacus](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.26237) [PP].
- Static vs live: static-benchmark scores about twice live-CTF scores — [CTFusion](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.11504) [PP].
- Task validity and outcome validity framing; CVE-Bench grader flaws changed scores by 32.5% and 10% — [ABC](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02825v5) [PP]. The checklist explicitly asks benchmarks to state capabilities measured and construct validity (R.5-R.6).
- General ML construct validity: 445 LLM benchmarks reviewed by 29 experts; roughly half measure abstract phenomena without clear definitions; only 16% used statistical tests; eight recommendations — Bean et al., [NeurIPS 2025 D&B](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/1967e0fc3aa6cbbace562f5cb8e3954e-Abstract-Datasets_and_Benchmarks_Track.html) [PR] (OII summary for the percentages: [link](https://intelligent-earth.ox.ac.uk/node/4752736)).
- CVE-Bench's motivation: CTF benchmarks' "vulnerabilities and tasks do not reflect real-world scenarios" — [arXiv 2503.17332v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.17332v4). Counter-critique: another paper calls CVE-Bench's scope and scale limited — [search summary](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.07830) (CyBiasBench; not read in full).
- Benchmarks are proxies, "not estimates of deployment risk"; results are mediated by scaffold, task construction, saturation and contamination — [arXiv 2607.25379v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.25379v2) [PP, July 2026; vendor-incident-based; one author coded sources].
- AISI caveats: ranges lack active defenders, no penalty for noisy actions, so "cannot determine whether Mythos Preview could attack well-defended systems" — [AISI](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/our-evaluation-of-claude-mythos-previews-cyber-capabilities).
- Anthropic: "a proof-of-concept only indicates that a bug is reproducible or reachable", motivating exploit-depth ladders — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/exploit-evals) [VENDOR]. ExploitBench critique: existing benchmarks treat a crash as exploitation success — [ExploitBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.14153).
- Task-specific gaps: CTF tasks isolate one skill whereas real attacks chain vulnerabilities (Meta Muse Spark report, per search snippet; [arXiv 2606.12429](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.12429) not read); a TU Wien thesis argues CTF-style benchmarks risk "insufficient representation of real-world scenarios" ([thesis PDF](https://repositum.tuwien.at/bitstream/20.500.12708/224568/1/Reinsperger%20Manuel%20-%202025%20-%20Dangerous%20Capability%20Evaluation%20of%20Large%20Language...pdf), snippet only, not peer-reviewed); PACEbench reports zero success on WAF-bypass tasks for all agents (via survey, [arXiv 2607.02605v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.02605v1)); the survey also cites CyberGym-E2E at 7.6% end-to-end vs 82.3% patch-only.
- Cheating and grading validity outside security: METR found at least 16% of successful 8h+ runs "illegitimate", dropped tasks for cheating, and notes only about half of SWE-Bench Verified passes would be accepted in code review — [METR](https://metr.org/blog/2026-05-19-frontier-risk-report/).
- Real-world field signals (not benchmark-correlated): Palo Alto Networks reports 26 CVEs (75 issues) from AI scanning across 130+ products vs a typical monthly volume under five CVEs; METR cites Claude Mythos Preview finding "thousands of vulnerabilities" as public anecdote — [CyberScoop](https://cyberscoop.com/ai-autonomous-cyber-capability-benchmarks-broken-gpt5-claude-mythos/), [METR](https://metr.org/blog/2026-05-19-frontier-risk-report/) [secondary/vendor].
- Search for correlation studies returned nothing: closest are BountyBench's dollar mapping and CyberGym's zero-day claims (34 zero-days), which tie a benchmark to real outcomes by construction but do not test predictive validity — [BountyBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15216v3), [CyberGym](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.02548).

### Inferences
- Because no paper pairs benchmark scores with field outcomes, any claim that "X% on Cybench/XBEN means the agent finds real bugs" is unvalidated; the evidence supports only "can solve prepared tasks with known solutions".
- The ctf-abacus result (rank correlation 0.88 on Cybench but -0.06 on HTB between headline and verified scores) shows that validity problems are benchmark-specific, so one benchmark's audit does not transfer.
- Seeded-vulnerable-app benchmarks (Juice Shop-like) have an additional validity problem not covered in a dedicated paper: the task is "find what is documented", which also conflates memory with discovery (inference from the Escape and EthiBench caveats).

### Gaps
- No predictive-validity or criterion-validity study for security-agent benchmarks found (benchmark score vs bounty payout, vs human pentest findings, vs field CVEs). This is a literature gap per my searches, not proof of absence.
- Muse Spark, TU Wien thesis and CyBiasBench statements are from search snippets or partial reads.

## 5. Recall-against-ground-truth designs (documented vulnerability lists instead of flags)

### Takeaway
A small set of 2026 papers and vendor reports score agents against an annotated list of an application's vulnerabilities; ground truth is hand-curated, tends to be incomplete, and false-positive handling is the weak point (unlisted real bugs are scored as FPs). Reported designs: LLM-judge bipartite matching (EthiBench), differential patched/unpatched execution (BountyBench, ExploitBench), manual validation of every finding (Doyensec, Escape), heuristic precision (dfbench), heuristic negatives (SastBench).

### Cited Findings
- EthiBench: JSONL ground truth curated by security researchers from disclosed vulnerabilities in the target repos "through an iterative process of multiple experiments and application analysis"; no annotator counts or inter-annotator agreement; LLM matcher (GPT 5.4 Mini) chosen after a 50-finding check (25 TP, 25 FP) among four models, 3 replicates, about 1 misclassification on average; unmatched findings count as FPs and the paper warns real omitted vulnerabilities may be scored as FPs, recommending periodic expert review; duplicates tracked separately; cumulative multi-run evaluation shows FP accumulation lowering F1 — [arXiv HTML v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.10834v1). Blog adds: string matching fails, static answer keys score missed real findings as FP, single runs mislead — [Ethiack blog](https://ethiack.com/info-hub/research/ai-pentesting-benchmarks-are-so-bad-we-made-a-new-one) [VENDOR].
- dfbench v1: 253 examples, 910 vulnerabilities, 27 weakness classes, 17 languages from public disclosures; precision estimated heuristically (a file with a known bug is a "hotspot"; if no new reports appear within 9 months of a fix on 10k+-star repos, the file is treated as likely clean); out-of-ground-truth findings get human review; first manual audit differed from the heuristic by under 5%, called "not foolproof", reported as a lower bound; authors say recall-only optimisation encourages overreporting and that traditional datasets score valid extra findings as FPs — [dfbench v1](https://depthfirst.com/research/dfbench-v1) [VENDOR; scores only in charts].
- BountyBench: Detect counts only if the exploit works on the vulnerable snapshot and fails on the patched one (Detect Indicator); authors manually reproduced each bounty, wrote exploit, patch and code/runtime invariants, ran invariants repeatedly to remove flaky ones, cross-reviewed; limitations: human-written exploits and invariants may miss things; Detect limited to vulnerabilities already added to the systems; adding systems takes up to tens of hours each — [arXiv 2505.15216v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15216v3).
- ExploitBench: deterministic oracles (llvm-cov, differential binaries, challenge-response builtins, nonce round-trip), three randomised rounds with unanimity, no LLM judge; unanimity undercredits flaky exploits — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.14153).
- Doyensec (consultancy; sponsored by Aikido, disclosed; Aikido "had input into the constraints"): two apps (Fider, Photoview) chosen by `$RANDOM%442` from a curated list of 442 web apps; every finding manually validated as TP or FP by Doyensec staff; scans run 2026-03-23 to 2026-04-21; Aikido 49 validated findings, XBOW 31 (Fider: Aikido 17, XBOW 24; Photoview: 32 and 7 per Escape's table); limitations: only two apps, one configuration per platform — [Doyensec PDF](https://doyensec.com/resources/ComparingAIApplicationSecurityTestingPlatforms_Doyensec.pdf) (text extracted locally). This is the only third-party-style measurement of two commercial tools; it has no preset ground-truth list (validation is post hoc per finding, so recall is relative, not absolute).
- Escape.tech: manual validation, same validation standard and severity assigner, raw TP counts plus severity-weighted score (Critical/High x5, Medium x2, Low x1); reports FP rates of 0% for Cascade (0/94 findings), 4% Aikido (2/49), 3% XBOW (1/31); the vendor designed the benchmark and ran its own product, and Cascade findings were Escape-validated — [Escape blog](https://escape.tech/blog/modern-ai-powered-pentesting-tools-in-depth-benchmark/) [VENDOR]. Other vendors (Invicti, Strobes, Astra, Hacktron) reran the Fider/Photoview pair with their own tools — [search results](https://www.invicti.com/blog/security-labs/invicti-agentic-pentest-benchmark-fider-photoview) [VENDOR, not read].
- SastBench: negatives are heuristic (Semgrep findings with no CWE overlap with the commit's CVE, minus findings in the same function as a known vulnerability); authors accept that some FPs may be real; precision and recall both reported with MCC — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.02941).
- PentestEval: 346 tasks over 12 vulnerable scenarios with expert-annotated ground truth, stage-level evaluation; 9 LLMs; end-to-end pipelines 31% success (survey cites "below 6%" for the same paper, conflicting) — [arXiv 2512.14233](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.14233) [PP], [survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.02605v1).
- Ground-truth label quality in vulnerability datasets generally: ICSE 2023 study of four datasets found label inaccuracy and duplication at rates the abstract gives as 20-71% inaccurate and 17-99% duplicated (a mirror noted possible garbling; check the PDF) — Croft et al., [arXiv 2301.05456](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.05456v1) [PR].
- Scoring bugs that corrupt ground truth: CVE-Bench grader accepted spurious proof (ABC) — [ABC](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02825v5); ABC checklist items T.5 (isolate agents from ground truth), T.7-T.10 (verify ground-truth annotations, provide an oracle solver, inspect outliers).

### Inferences
- The unlisted-real-bug problem is acknowledged by EthiBench, dfbench and SastBench; none supplies a quantified audit of ground-truth completeness (annotator-miss rate), so reported precision is a lower bound and recall is relative to an incomplete list.
- The strongest ground-truth designs for exploitation (BountyBench, ExploitBench) use patched/unpatched differential oracles; for web-app finding recall the field mostly relies on manual or LLM-judge matching, which adds judge error (EthiBench validated on only 50 findings).
- Replication is thin: EthiBench 3 runs, SastBench 1 run, Doyensec 2 apps, ExploitBench 3 seeds; these designs lack the statistical power the ABC checklist (R.10) asks for.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed paper found on recall-vs-annotated-list evaluation of web pentest agents; EthiBench is a vendor-authored preprint, dfbench a vendor blog.
- Inter-annotator agreement and audit procedures for ground truth are not reported in EthiBench; dfbench's audit details are only summarised.
- I did not open the Invicti, Strobes, Astra or Hacktron reports.

## 6. Reporting standards, checklists and preregistration norms

### Takeaway
There are general checklists (ABC for agentic benchmarks, BetterBench, Bean et al.) that cover contamination, validity and statistics, and Happe and Cito's review gives security-specific testbed and experiment-design recommendations; I found no preregistration norm or security-agent-specific reporting standard.

### Cited Findings
- Agentic Benchmark Checklist (ABC; Zhu et al., 25 authors, arXiv 2507.02825 v5, 2025-08-07) [PP, venue not stated]: task validity items T.1-T.10, outcome validity O.a-O.i, reporting R.1-R.13 (open datasets/harness, contamination measures, stated capabilities and construct validity, documented mitigations, impact of limitations, statistical significance, interpretation guidelines, non-AI and trivial-agent baselines); applied to 10 benchmarks (SWE-bench, SWE-Lancer, KernelBench, BIRD, Cybench, MLE-bench, GAIA, tau-bench, WebArena, OSWorld): 7 with task-validity flaws, 7 with outcome-validity flaws, all 10 with reporting limitations; performance errors up to 100% relative; CVE-Bench overestimation reduced by 33% — [ABC](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02825v5).
- BetterBench (Reuel et al.): 46 criteria over the benchmark lifecycle applied to 24 benchmarks, minimum-quality checklist, most benchmarks lack statistical significance reporting and replicability, living assessments site — [NeurIPS 2024 D&B](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/26889e8359e7ef8a7f5d77457364ca55-Abstract.html) [PR spotlight], [site](https://betterbench.stanford.edu/index.html). Whether any cyber benchmark is among the 24 assessed: not checked.
- Bean et al.: eight recommendations on defining the phenomenon, task selection and scoring — [NeurIPS 2025 D&B](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/1967e0fc3aa6cbbace562f5cb8e3954e-Abstract-Datasets_and_Benchmarks_Track.html) [PR]; the eight recommendations were not extracted.
- Security-specific: Happe and Cito (TU Wien), review of 19 papers/18 prototypes (2023 to May 2025): recommend at least one state-of-the-art, one open-weight and possibly a small model, document configuration such as temperature, flag contamination and CTF-unrepresentativeness; reproducibility of baselines poor — [arXiv 2504.10112](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.10112) [PP] (details via search summary).
- ctf-abacus proposes reporting provenance alongside headline scores — [arXiv 2608.26237](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.26237) [PP].
- Eval-containment/protocol recommendations (record permitted target set, egress testing, telemetry, stop rule) — [arXiv 2607.25379v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.25379v2) [PP]. This is the relevant source for a scope-guard style norm, but it is not a benchmark reporting standard.
- Survey recommendation: standard protocols for separating training and evaluation tasks; partial-credit metrics — [arXiv 2607.02605v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.02605v1) [PP].
- Practices visible in 2026 papers: three-seed reporting and unanimity oracles (ExploitBench), Welch t-tests with Cohen's d on 3 runs (EthiBench), cumulative multi-run and temporal evaluation (EthiBench), best-of-N and elicitation configs (3CB). Samples are small throughout.
- Preregistration: I found no mention of it in any source; no security-benchmark paper in this review states a preregistered hypothesis or analysis plan.

### Inferences
- A defensible minimal reporting set for a security-agent result can be assembled from ABC R-items plus security specifics: per-target results with n runs and CIs, harness and token budget, model snapshot and date, pass@k definition, flag-vs-finding scoring, provenance audit of solves, public/private target split with a contamination caveat, and vendor-conflict disclosure. This is my synthesis; no source publishes it as a standard.
- Vendor-evaluating-own-product conflicts are rarely disclosed in the paper itself (AIRTBench makes no disclosure; Doyensec and EthiBench do disclose affiliation or sponsorship), and none of the checklists reviewed has an explicit conflict-of-interest item.

### Gaps
- No preregistration practice found for security-agent benchmarks (search-based absence).
- I did not read BetterBench's 46 criteria or the eight Bean et al. recommendations in full, and did not check whether BetterBench scored any cyber benchmark.
- Did not search for the Inspect/AISI evaluation-reporting guidance or the METR eval-reporting guidance beyond the pages cited.
